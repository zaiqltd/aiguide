There is real money in AI for a South African business. There is also a lot of it getting wasted on the
wrong things: tools that look impressive in a demo, solve a problem you do not have, and quietly bill you
every month. This is the honest take, from building these systems, on what is overhyped, what is worth
paying for, and how to tell the difference before you spend.

## The one test

Before you pay for any AI tool, ask one question: does it save real time or money on a task you actually
do, repeatedly? Not a task you might do one day. Not a clever capability you would enjoy showing a friend.
A task that is already on your plate, often. If the answer is no, you are looking at a shiny object, and a
shiny object costs you the subscription, the setup time, and the attention it pulls off the work that pays.

<Callout variant="take">
Most wasted AI spend fails this single test. The tool is genuinely impressive and completely irrelevant to
anything you do twice a week. Useful AI is boring: it sits on a task you already hate doing and quietly
does the dull part. If you cannot name the recurring task it saves you, do not pay for it.
</Callout>

## What's usually overhyped

These are patterns, not named products. The category is the problem, not any one brand.

- **Thin "AI" wrappers.** A prompt with a logo and a monthly fee. You are paying a markup for something the
  underlying model does directly, and the wrapper adds little but a nicer button.
- **Tools that need more setup and maintenance than they save.** If it takes a week to wire in and constant
  babysitting to keep working, the time you spend feeding it usually outweighs the time it gives back.
- **Chasing every new launch.** There is a shiny new model or tool every week. Switching to each one burns
  hours relearning workflows for a marginal gain you will not notice on your actual numbers.
- **Generic AI-written content.** Publishing unedited AI text that reads exactly like everyone else's. It
  does not just fail to help; it tells customers you did not care enough to add anything of your own.
- **"AI agents" trusted with consequential tasks.** Handing an agent your money, contracts or customer
  decisions and walking away. They are confident even when wrong, and they are not reliable enough yet to
  own work where a mistake is costly.
- **Paying for enterprise tiers you do not need.** Buying the big plan for one or two people when the free
  or cheap tier does the job. You are paying for scale, seats and compliance you do not have.
- **Buying AI for a problem you do not actually have.** The most expensive kind. The tool works perfectly
  and solves nothing, because the problem it fixes was never on your plate.

## What's genuinely worth it

The flip side is real. AI earns its keep on a handful of jobs, and these are worth paying for.

- **Drafting and summarising.** First drafts of emails, proposals, posts and documents, and quick summaries
  of long threads or reports. The draft is nearly free; you keep the editing and the judgement.
- **Customer-reply assistance.** Helping you answer the same questions faster and more consistently,
  especially after hours. A human still owns anything sensitive or unusual.
- **The boring half of bookkeeping, with human review.** Sorting, categorising and flagging the routine
  grind, with a person checking the output before it counts. AI does the tedium; you keep the accountability.
- **Getting found by AI search.** As more people ask an AI instead of scrolling a results page, being the
  business it quotes is worth real effort. This one compounds, and it is where a lot of the upside now sits.

The thread through all four: AI takes the repetitive, checkable part, and a human keeps the call. That is
exactly where it pays, and exactly the line the overhyped tools cross when they ask you to trust them with
the judgement too.

## How to test before you pay

You do not need to guess, and you should not buy on the strength of a demo. Run it on one real task for a
week. Pick something you genuinely do often, use the tool for it, and track roughly how much time it saves
you. At the end of the week you will know. If it clearly earns its keep, keep it. If it is marginal, or you
found yourself fighting it, cancel it. Do this for everything, and review your subscriptions every so often:
a tool that earned its place six months ago may not be earning it now. Cancel anything that does not.
